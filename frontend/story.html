Day 00 · the wrong door

We almost built something that already existed.

The first idea was a wallet-native trust layer — soulbound attestation badges, a mock attester, a from-scratch registry. It sounded original. It wasn't. A crawl of GIWA's own GitHub showed they had already shipped Dojang: verified-address, verified-balance and verified-code schemas, an Upbit attester, an EAS indexer. All of it, live on testnet.

Walk that into a demo day and the judges — who are the GIWA team — say "that's Dojang, we built it." Instant fail on originality. So the strategy flipped, and the flip was the insight: don't build the trust layer. Build the first thing that makes the trust layer worth having. Dojang was infrastructure with no applications. We became the applications.

Day 01 · a new toolchain

First day in an unfamiliar domain reads like a comedy of errors.

The founder had spent 17+ years in industrial-automation BD & GTM and, over the last year, shipped two products — a real-time behavioural engine and a compliance API. But he had never touched a Solidity toolchain. So day one looked the way it looks for anyone crossing into a new domain: the first command, mkdir -p, failed, because -p is a Mac/Linux flag and the machine runs Windows. Then npx hardhat init pulled Hardhat 3, a ground-up rewrite where none of the planned config works; we nuked node_modules and pinned to Hardhat 2. The pin was too low, tripping an ERESOLVE peer-dependency wall; hardhat@2 cleared it. The wallet — Rabby — wouldn't pop up, because the page was opened from file:// and extensions don't inject there; a local http-server fixed it.

And then the server, restarted from the project root, served an "Index of /" directory listing instead of the app — which also meant the testnet .env was being offered on the local network. Harmless (testnet key, no real money), but it taught the one rule that matters for hosting: serve and deploy only the frontend folder, never the root. Every failure was a 30-second fix. The question was never "can he build" — he'd already built, twice. The question was whether the on-chain build would be honest.

Day 02 · the 1e21

The vault ate a deposit and the price went to space.

Deposit 100 TKRW. Position reads 0.00. The vault's TVL reads 1194. The share price reads 1.0945e+21. And the "run strategy" button pops "deposit into the vault first" — while 1194 TKRW sits right there. Four symptoms, one pathology: OpenZeppelin's ERC-4626 ships without its inflation-protection switch on by default. With it off, an empty vault can take your assets and mint you zero shares — money in the box, no receipt. Price = trapped assets ÷ ~0 shares = astronomical. The button's "any shareholders?" check saw a supply of zero and refused, even though the box was full of ownerless dust.

This is a famous exploit — the inflation / donation / first-depositor attack — and it has drained real vaults. We didn't hit it because we were attacked; we hit it because the default is a footgun. The fix: redeploy with _decimalsOffset()=18 (the Morpho / Euler v2 / OpenZeppelin mitigation), and harden the reader so a corrupted price renders as — and a red "degraded" pill instead of lying. The old vault was abandoned; the ~100 TKRW of testnet dust written off as tuition.

Day 03 · the wrong ruler

The vault was fine. Our measuring tape wasn't.

After the redeploy the price still showed — and the state still read "degraded." The contract was correct — the position now showed a real 1002.48, which a broken vault can't produce. The bug was in the reader: the inflation offset makes shares 1e18× more granular, so a meaningful "one share" is 1e36 wei, not 1e18. We were asking the contract for the value of a sliver, getting a sliver, and our own safety clamp correctly refused to print it. Fix the unit → price snaps to 1.010038 → state flips to jade healthy → and we added a delta chip (+1.00%) so the accrued yield is visible at a glance, plus a self-healing cost basis so the green P&L finally appears.

Two rounds of "the dashboard says something impossible," two rounds of tracing it to a real cause. The dashboard wasn't decoration — it was the instrument that caught both bugs.

Day 03 · the ethical question

"Wait — is this a Ponzi?"

The founder looked at a working vault and asked the question most builders skip until after they're rekt — and asked it not as a newcomer, but as someone in crypto since 2011, through Mt.Gox and every cycle since. That isn't naivety. That's scar tissue. The answer is a clean test, now printed on the product: freeze all new deposits — do existing holders still earn? Yes → real yield (the cash flow is external). No → scheme. ERC-4626 accrual passes structurally: the price moves only when external value enters — a borrower's interest, a trader's fee, a validator's reward, a treasury's coupon. On testnet that source is a button; on mainnet it's KRW sovereign yield, regulated and Sybil-proof.

The thing he sensed — "early in, early paid; latecomers lose" — is real, but it's not a business model, it's the inflation attack above, and it's already patched. So the nose was right; the diagnosis just needed the library's name attached. From there came the doctrine: study the survivors (Yearn, Morpho, Euler v2, Aave, Lido, RWA vaults), take isolation + the inflation offset + roles + TWAP oracles + MEV-aware harvest, subtract shared liquidity and spot oracles and slow governance. That study didn't become a rebuild — the deadline forbids it — it became the roadmap, and the slide that proves the founder understands production risk.
